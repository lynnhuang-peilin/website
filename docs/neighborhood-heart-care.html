Beating Cancer Is Step One.
Seeing a Heart Doctor Shouldn’t Depend on Your ZIP Code.

Some life-saving breast cancer treatments can quietly strain the heart. Our new study of 2,637 older women found that the neighborhood a patient lives in helps determine whether — and how soon — she gets the follow-up heart care she may need.

By Pei-Lin Huang, with Manu M. Mysore, Brian Barr and Eberechukwu Onukwugha
University of Maryland, Baltimore

Published in Clinical Breast Cancer, February 2026 · A plain-language, interactive summary

Breast cancer treatment works — survival keeps improving. But three of the most effective treatments can quietly strain the heart. Doctors call it cardiotoxicity. It is treatable, and often preventable, if someone is watching.

Treatment begins Anthracycline chemo, HER2-targeted drugs, or radiation near the chest — all proven against cancer
→
The heart feels it These treatments can weaken the heart muscle — often silently at first
→
Watching works Caught early by a cardiologist, heart strain can usually be managed or reversed

Heart disease — not cancer — is the No. 1 cause of death among breast cancer survivors.
That’s why a new field, cardio-oncology, exists: heart doctors and cancer doctors working together.

So we asked a simple question: after starting one of these treatments, do older women actually get in to see a doctor who can watch their heart? And does it matter where they live?

Two Women, One Diagnosis

Same cancer. Same treatment. Different neighborhoods.

M

“Margaret,” 73

Lives near a grocery store and several clinics

Diagnosed with breast cancer, Margaret starts a HER2-targeted drug. Her oncologist refers her to a cardiologist, who checks her heart within two months and adjusts her blood-pressure medication.

✓ Heart monitored early — problems can be caught while they are still reversible

R

“Ruth,” 74

Lives in a food desert & doctor-shortage area

Ruth starts the same drug. The nearest cardiologist is over an hour away by two buses. Her area has too few primary care doctors. Months pass. Her mild shortness of breath goes unchecked.

✗ In our study, women in areas like Ruth’s had 22–34% lower odds of an early heart-doctor visit

Margaret and Ruth are fictional, composite illustrations — not real patients. The statistics are real.

This number alone surprised us. These were not low-risk patients: nearly all had high blood pressure, a third had heart failure, and 7 in 10 were already taking heart medications when their cancer treatment began. By European cardiology society standards, most would warrant a cardio-oncology referral. Yet fewer than half saw a cardiologist in the critical first three months.

So who did get in the door? Comparing the 799 women who saw a cardiologist early with the 1,838 who didn’t reveals a clear pattern: the system responds to what’s already in the medical chart.

In one sense, that is the system working: medical need predicts specialist care, and the women on HER2-targeted therapies — drugs with explicit heart-checkup guidelines — were several times more likely to see a cardiologist. But medical need is only half the story. When we looked at where the women lived — using their census tract, a neighborhood-sized area defined by the U.S. Census — the picture sharpened.

“The same two things that make it hard to eat well — no grocery store, no nearby doctor — also predicted who got follow-up heart care.”What the data showed

Odds tell you whether a visit happened. We also measured how long women waited. The gap between primary care and heart care was striking.

Going Deeper

It’s not one thing. It’s the neighborhood as a whole.

Neighborhood disadvantages travel in packs — a place with no supermarket often also has fewer doctors, older housing and less transit. So we let the data group our 18 factors into clusters (a technique called factor analysis). Two emerged:

The takeaway in one sentence: the neighborhood itself — not just the patient — predicted the delay, even after accounting for age, cancer stage and other illnesses.

Why It Matters

A risk calculator that knows your chart — but not your bus route

Before heart-straining treatment starts, doctors use risk checklists to decide who needs closer monitoring. Those checklists read the medical chart — blood pressure, age, heart history. They never ask about the bus route. Our findings suggest one added question could change that:

“How hard is it for you to get to appointments and groceries?”
One screening question could flag the patients most likely to fall through the cracks.

Proactive referrals Flagged patients get a cardio-oncology referral before problems start — not after
Telehealth options Video check-ins when the nearest cardiologist is an hour away
Transportation help Ride programs and visit bundling, arranged by oncology social workers
Food prescriptions Produce-prescription programs have lowered blood pressure and food insecurity

What this study can say

  • Neighborhood conditions were strongly linked to whether and when heart care happened
  • The links held after adjusting for age, cancer stage and other illnesses
  • It fits a larger body of evidence tying neighborhood disadvantage to worse heart outcomes in cancer survivors

What it can’t say

  • It can’t prove cause and effect — it’s observational
  • It covered insured women 66+; younger or uninsured patients may face steeper barriers
  • Some heart monitoring inside oncology visits isn’t visible in claims data
What You Can Do

Five questions worth asking your cancer team

“Could my treatment affect my heart?”Ask specifically whether your regimen includes anthracyclines, HER2-targeted drugs, or chest radiation.
“Should I see a cardiologist or a cardio-oncology clinic — and when?”Many centers now have clinics dedicated to heart health during cancer treatment. A referral early on is reasonable to request, especially if you have blood pressure, diabetes or heart history.
“What heart symptoms should make me call you?”New shortness of breath, swelling in the legs, unusual fatigue, palpitations or chest discomfort deserve a call — don’t wait for the next scheduled visit.
“Can any of this be done closer to home — or by video?”If travel is the barrier, ask about telehealth check-ins, local labs, or coordinating heart checks with existing oncology visits.
“Is there help with transportation or food?”Oncology social workers and navigators can often connect you with ride programs, food assistance and other services. You have to ask — so ask.

About the study

Huang P-L, Mysore MM, Barr B, Onukwugha E. Relationship Between Census Tract-Level Social Determinants of Health and Cardiovascular Care among Individuals Diagnosed With Breast Cancer. Clinical Breast Cancer. 2026;26(2):191–203. Open access: doi.org/10.1016/j.clbc.2025.08.020

In one sentence: Using national cancer-registry data linked to Medicare claims for 2,637 older women, we found that neighborhood conditions — food access, doctor supply and community infrastructure — predicted whether and how quickly women received heart care after starting potentially cardiotoxic breast cancer treatment.

Methods, briefly

Retrospective cohort, SEER-Medicare 2007–2018, women 66+ with invasive breast cancer who received potentially cardiotoxic treatment within 12 months of diagnosis. Census-tract social determinants from PolicyMap; 21 variables guided by the Healthy People 2030 framework, plus two empirically derived domains from exploratory factor analysis. Logistic regression and cause-specific Cox models adjusted for patient- and tract-level factors. Approved by the University of Maryland Baltimore IRB.
